## 1. Candidate items

### A. The lecture's narrative arc (physics page, possibly "the lecture")

**A1 ★★★ The opening question.** Used in every script.
> "If a tree falls in the forest and no one is around to hear it, does it actually make a sound? … What this question is really asking is if the tree's existence is dependent on being perceived. Does something become less real or not real at all if no one is watching it? … Can we decide this question experimentally?" (LINZ-EN slide 2; ALP slide 2 has Benjamin's version: "you are very welcome to give me your thoughts on this after the performance")

The CIVA notes add the moon version ("Moon example: look away, is it still there?"). VIE-2 (slides 15–16) pairs it with Einstein's
> "Do you really believe the moon exists only when you are looking at it?"

→ **/physics/ opening.** The site's physics page starts with dice. This is
the question the whole lecture is built to answer, and it's how audiences
first met the project. The Einstein moon quote is a natural pull-quote.

**A2 ★★ Classical physics as a clockwork.** (LINZ-EN slide 3, identical in ALP and PAL)
> "The physics we knew around the year 1900 had four big pillars: … Mechanics & Acoustics … Optics … Electrodynamics … Thermodynamics. All those theories … have common characteristics: Objects have well defined properties at all times; Everything has a cause; Randomness is only due to our ignorance of exact initial conditions; They are deterministic (even in chaos). Therefore, in classical physics, the universe is a deterministic clockwork."

Followed by (LINZ-EN slide 4, spoken by Haslinger, then Orthner in ALP):
> "So if you know from all the atoms, their position and speed, then you can calculate back into the past, but also predict the future! Everything can be predicted, including our thoughts and all our actions. … there is no free will!"

and the "few small clouds" (the sun, the stability of atoms) that led to
quantum mechanics.

→ /physics/, chapter 1 ("Chance"), as the lead-in before subjective versus
objective randomness. The four pillars suit a small typographic list.

**A3 ★★ Subjective versus objective randomness, the roulette version.** (LINZ-EN slide 5)
> "In classical physics, seemingly random events – like the final position of a roulette ball – are in fact not really random. They are just too complicated to be calculated. … We call this randomness subjective. In quantum mechanics, randomness seems to be objective. Whether a photon … gets reflected or transmitted at a beam splitter, is objectively random with 50:50 odds. There is no cause for the individual event."

VIE-2 appendix slide "Randomness in Nature" puts it as a two-column table:
classical (roulette, weather), "randomness is merely subjective"; quantum
(radioactive decay, photon at a 50/50 beam splitter), "randomness is objective".

→ /physics/ chapter 1. The site already says this; the roulette example and
the two-column table are crisper. Note the script's hedge "seems to be
objective" (see §2).

**A4 ★★ The first and second quantum revolutions.** (LINZ-EN slides 6–7; VIE-2 slide 9 as a summary)
> "Quantum mechanics gave rise to the … first quantum revolution … The transistor … Lasers … magnetic resonance imaging … Today, we are living in the second quantum revolution. … Information itself and the way we process it is quantum. … quantum bits or qubits. … a quantum computer from Innsbruck built by the team of Martin Ringbauer, where the qubits are ions in a vacuum chamber … a satellite-based quantum internet."
> "Quantum physics is not just a fashion, it is something, which is real, and it opens new mind blowing possibilities – also for the arts." (LINZ-EN slide 7)

DPG (slides 9–13) reframes this as a history of music following technology:
"The Electronic Age — Synthesizers and Engineered Randomness", "The Digital
Age (First Quantum Revolution)", "The Second Quantum Revolution — Harnessing
Quantum Effects Themselves … Where is the art and music?"

→ About or landing, as the "why now" context (the paper's Afterword says the
same). The DPG music-and-technology arc is the most original version and fits
an about text or the /pieces/ intro. It also ties to Martin Ringbauer's
research on /people/.

**A5 ★★★ The conductor and light.** The project's core metaphor, in every script. (LINZ-EN slide 9)
> "Entanglement is about correlations. Our world, our life is also about correlations. Correlations in time, getting up in the morning, meeting people at a certain time in a certain place. Music is about correlations and coordination. A conductor … coordinates and correlates musicians. He or she interacts with the musician through light. One needs to see the conductor. In a pitch-dark room, nobody can see the conductor's commands, so they would be meaningless."
> CIVA notes: "Tonight, light will be conductor."
> DPG brainstorm: "Entanglement is all about correlations. An Orchestra is also all about correlations."

→ **Landing/about and /physics/.** The site uses "quantum conductor" but never
explains why a conductor, or that a conductor works through light. It's the
most elegant bridge between physics and music the team has, and should be the
hinge of the landing "What this is" copy.

**A6 ★★★ Music instructions as hidden variables: a musical Bell argument.** (VIE-2 slides 19–22)
> "Bell's Theorem. Assume: 'Realism' – Instruments make sounds even when nobody is listening. 'Locality' – The choice Alice makes should not change the sound of Bob. S = ⟨A1B1⟩ + ⟨A1B2⟩ + ⟨A2B1⟩ – ⟨A2B2⟩ ≤ 2" (slide 19)
> "Example: A conductor sends instructions. For Alice: if volume → loud, if timbre → wooden. For Bob: if volume → loud, if timbre → metallic. … A1 = +1 (loud) B1 = +1 (loud) A1B1 = +1 … A2B2 = −1. The 4th correlation value is determined by the first 3: A1B1 + A1B2 + A2B1 − A2B2 = 2 … One cannot exceed the value 2." (slides 21–22)
> LINZ-OLD slide 13: "Instruments make sounds that are determined by the conductor. The choice Alice makes should not change the sound of Bob."

→ **/physics/ "Bell's test".** This is the site's "why S ≤ 2" proof told
entirely in the project's own language (a conductor, two musicians, loud or
soft, wooden or metallic) instead of abstract ±1s. It could replace or head
the current 16-case table: show the conductor's instruction sheet and let the
visitor try to beat 2. This is the best single find in the decks.

**A7 ★★ "Entangled musicians": classical expectation versus quantum reality.** (VIE-2 slide 14)
The slide shows Alice choosing an instrument (drum or guitar icons) and Bob a
volume (loud or soft), with the captions "classical expectation: sometimes
both" and "quantum reality: never both". It is a Hardy-style picture.
→ **Only with care** (see §2): it describes an idealised Hardy-type state, not
the CHSH measurement the project performs. At most, use it as a simplified
illustration clearly labelled "a thought experiment". A6 is safer.

**A8 ★★ Einstein, EPR and the steam engine.** (LINZ-EN slides 11–12)
> "He did not believe in objective randomness and famously said that he is convinced that god 'does not throw dice' … He called that 'spooky action at a distance' to mock this effect which he found unreasonable."
> "Just as the working of a steam engine can be reduced to the movement of all the water molecules in the steam, so could the random events in quantum physics be reduced to yet-unknown and hidden variables."

→ /physics/ "Entanglement": the steam-engine picture is a good companion to
the gloves analogy the page already uses.

**A9 ★★ The DPG intuitive Bell argument with Malus's law.** (DPG hidden slides 43–49, note: "Non rigurous explanation just to remind ourselves of the flavour of the bell experiment")
The slide sequence: two polarisers at 45° pass 100% → 50%; at 22.5° pass
cos²(22.5°) ≈ 85%; chaining two 22.5° steps gives cos⁴(22.5°) ≈ 76% versus
cos²(45°) = 50%, ending in "N(B) > N(A, B) + N(Ax)" and "'Hidden Variables'".
→ ★ at most, as an optional visual deep-dive on /physics/. It is explicitly
non-rigorous. The interactive BellLab already does this job better.

## 2. Accuracy risks

Check each of these against `docs/physics.md` → "Things the site must NOT
claim" before using the item.

1. **"If A is ↕, then B is ↔. If A is ↔, then B is ↕."** (every script, VIE-2
   slides 10–11) **contradicts the setup.** The source makes |Φ⁺⟩ = (|HH⟩+|VV⟩)/√2
   (in practice it behaves like Φ⁻, which is also *equal* in H/V). The paper says
   "each photon will give a random result … but both photons certainly show the
   same result". The site must say **same**, not opposite.
2. **"The polarization does not exist before measurement."** This is an
   interpretation, strictly what Bell rules out *together with locality*. Soften
   to "has no definite value before it is measured, as far as any local
   explanation goes", or keep it attributed to the lecture.
3. **"Hidden variables are ruled out. The world is quantum." / "Local hidden
   variables are ruled out."** Say *local* hidden variables, and say the
   loophole-free experiments that settled it were done elsewhere (2015); our
   stage setup does not close loopholes.
4. **"…perfectly correlated, even if the photons are measured at distant
   locations."** "Perfectly" is the ideal; on stage the correlations are strong
   but imperfect (S ≈ 2.2–2.6, not 2.83). Our Alice and Bob are about 30 cm
   apart. DPG note: "Ideally spacelike separated. But we travel". Never imply
   space-like separation on stage, and don't use DPG hidden slide 50 ("space-like
   separated").
